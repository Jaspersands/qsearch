A Polynomial-Time Quantum Algorithm for the Dihedral
Coset Problem
DANIEL R. SIMON, Amazon Web Services, USA
We present a polynomial-time quantum algorithm for the Dihedral Coset Problem (DCP). The algorithm is
based on Regev’s polynomial-time reduction of the Dihedral Subgroup Problem (DSP) to the modular subset
sum problem [12], but uses a different technique to erase sample bits without use of a subset sum oracle. The
algorithm can thus combine with Regev’s [12] reduction of lattice problems to DCP, improved by Brakerski,
Kirshanova, Stehlé and Wen [3], to yield polynomial-time quantum algorithms for various lattice problems,
such as finding a polynomial-factor approximation to the shortest vector in an 𝑛-dimensional lattice (SVP),
and the “learning with errors” problem (LWE). The algorithm can tolerate a faulty sample rate as high as
                                                                                                           √
1/𝑂 (log 𝑛), allowing the algorithm-reduction combination to efficiently solve, for example, SVP with a 𝑛
                                                             √
polylog(𝑛) approximation factor, or LWE instances with 𝛼 = 𝑛 polylog(𝑛).

1    Introduction
1.1 Lattice-Based Cryptography
The discovery of quantum algorithms capable of efficiently attacking the most popular classical
public-key cryptosystems [14] has created interest in public-key cryptosystems that are resistant to
such quantum attacks. One promising approach is “lattice cryptography” [10], which is based on the
believed computational difficulty of various problems over lattices, such as the polynomial-factor
approximate Shortest Vector Problem (𝑝 (𝑛)-SVP). 𝑝 (𝑛)-SVP is the problem of finding a non-zero
vector in an 𝑛-dimensional lattice whose length is within a polynomial 𝑝 (𝑛) factor of the length
of the shortest non-zero vector in the lattice. The security of several lattice-based cryptosystems,
such as the Ajtai-Dwork cryptosystem [1] and “learning with errors” (LWE) [13], depends on the
hardness of 𝑝 (𝑛)-SVP. The best previously available algorithms—classical or quantum—for solving
𝑝 (𝑛)-SVP, such as BKZ ([4]), require fully exponential time.

1.2    The Dihedral Subgroup Problem
The Dihedral Subgroup Problem (DSP) [5] can be stated as follows: assume that a function 𝑓 is
constant on a subgroup 𝐻 of a dihedral group 𝐺, as well as on each of its cosets; the problem is to
identify 𝐻 . Ettinger and Høyer [5] showed that it suffices to solve the problem in the case where
the subgroup is of order 2. Applying the standard “sampling” method results in the following very
simple formulation of the problem, due to Regev [12]: given a source of random samples of the
superposition

arbitrarily for each sample, find 𝑑.

1.3    The Dihedral Coset Problem
The Dihedral Coset Problem (DCP) [12] is identical to Regev’s formulation of the DSP, but with a
faulty sample probability of 1/𝑎(𝑛) in the DSP samples—that is, with probability 1/𝑎(𝑛) that the
sample will consist of a random bit and random value, rather than the correct superposition. Regev
[12] showed a polynomial-time reduction from solving 𝑎(𝑛)-SVP to solving DCP on a group of
                         2
size 2𝑁 , where 𝑁 ≈ 2𝑛 . This quadratic dimension increase was later removed in an improved
reduction from LWE presented by Brakerski,   √   Kirshanova, Stehlé and Wen [3]. These reductions
both yield an approximation factor 𝑎(𝑛) 𝑛 polylog(𝑛), but at the cost of a 1/𝑎(𝑛) faulty sample
probability. Kuperberg’s

3    A Note on [7]
Gupte, Ragavan and Zhandry [7] recently presented a proof that the density matrix representing
the state of the above algorithm following step 2—assuming averaging over untouched portions
of the 𝑦𝑖 values—is exponentially close to that which would be produced if all the qubits 𝑏𝑖 were
traced out (i.e., measured and then discarded). They argue that this result implies that the above
algorithm can’t possibly produce a correct value of 𝑑𝑛 with non-negligible probability, since the
information about 𝑑𝑛 is encoded in the relationship between the phases of each value of ℎ, and
thus destroyed when all the 𝑏𝑖 ’s are traced out, collapsing ℎ to a single value.
   As it turns out, Lemmas 1, 2 and 3 are all completely compatible with the result in [7]—Lemmas 2
and 3, in particular, focus on the 𝜙 𝐵 portion of the state, which could in fact be traced out, reducing
it to a single value, without affecting the algorithm at all. Rather, it’s Lemma 4—–the claim that

, Vol. 1, No. 1, Article . Publication date: November 2026.
Polynomial-Time Quantum Algorithm for DCP                                                                     11


the 𝜙𝐴 state portions are roughly evenly distributed among 𝑧 ∗ values—that fails once the bits in 𝜙𝐴
are traced out.
   Now, this would certainly cause the algorithm to fail—set 𝐴 is exactly the set that needs to
be represented for both values of ℎ and ℎ ∗ , and hence not be traced out, for the algorithm to
work. However, the fact that 𝐴 is post-selected such that the results of the Hadamard transforms on
its bits are all zero allows the states of ℎ and ℎ ∗ computed in the algorithm—perhaps surprisingly—to
diverge non-negligibly from the case where the values of the 𝑏𝑖 ’s in 𝐴 are traced out, despite the
theorem proven in [7].
   Consider, for example, the case where all the 𝑏𝑖 ’s are measured as zero following their Hadamard
transforms. (Or alternatively, consider a variation of the algorithm in which a random set 𝐴 of
𝑛/(log 𝑛) groups of 𝑏𝑖 ’s is pre-selected, then the rest of the 𝑏𝑖 ’s are measured, hoping that the
Hadamard transforms on set 𝐴 all result in zeroes.) In that case, the algorithm can easily be seen to
work correctly (as a mildly modified version of Regev’s original algorithm, with the 𝑏𝑖 ’s just erased
a different way), even though its pre-Hadamard-transform density matrix, per [7], is only negligibly
different from the one for the case where the 𝑏𝑖 ’s were all traced out, preventing the algorithm
from working correctly. Of course, the Hadamard transform on the 𝑏𝑖 ’s (or in the variation, the 𝑏𝑖 ’s
in 𝐴) only results in all zeroes with exponentially small probability. But note that the probability
expressed in the density matrix in [7] is over choices of the 𝑦𝑖 ’s, and an all-zeroes outcome of
the Hadamard transforms makes the average output across all 𝑦𝑖 ’s completely different from the
all-𝑏𝑖 ’s-traced-out case. In effect, the Hadamard transform concentrates the expected difference
between the intact-𝑏𝑖 ’s and traced-out-𝑏𝑖 ’s cases in the all-zeroes outcome, making it non-negligible
when conditioned on that outcome occurring.
   (In density matrix terms, the effect of the negligibly-different density matrices on the states of
the computed values ℎ ∗ and ℎ normally carries through the Hadamard transform because the states
whose amplitudes are summed have varying phases, and hence interfere negatively, keeping the
difference small. But this effect breaks down when the result of the Hadamard transform is all
zeroes, and therefore imparts no phases to the input states, causing the two density matrices, when
ℎ ∗ and ℎ are included, to diverge significantly.)
   This exceptional behavior of the all-zeroes Hadamard transform result is important because the
crux of the argument in [7] is the implicit claim that the negligible difference between the actual
and traced-out density matrices over choices of the untouched portions of the 𝑦𝑖 ’s is persistently
uniform and completely independent of the evolution of the algorithm’s state. But this claim is in
fact incorrect, as we’ve shown, since the difference can become non-negligible in the case of an
all-zeroes Hadamard transform result on a given set 𝐴 of 𝑏𝑖 ’s. Moreover, this case is recognizeable
to the algorithm, and can even be amplified and exploited by it. And that’s precisely what the
algorithm does by post-selecting 𝐴 to have an all-zeroes Hadamard transform result: the negligible
probability of this event occurring—and the density matrix thus diverging from the traced-out case,
yielding a correct algorithm output—for any particular choice of 𝐴 instead becomes a near-certainty
when 𝐴 is post-selected based on the outcome of the Hadamard transforms on the 𝑏𝑖 ’s.
   More precisely, consider another variation of the algorithm, identical to the previous one in
which 𝐴 is randomly pre-selected, except that we omit the measurement of the 𝑏𝑖 ’s in set 𝐵 (still
hoping that the Hadamard transforms on the 𝑏𝑖 ’s in 𝐴 all result in zeroes). The proof presented in
Section 2 can easily be adapted to show that this variation of the algorithm works correctly in the
(exponentially unlikely) case that the Hadamard transforms on the 𝑏𝑖 ’s in the pre-selected 𝐴 do
in fact all produce zeroes. (The proof is slightly simpler than for the full algorithm—for example,
the claims of pairwise independence in Lemma 3 can be over choices of 𝐴 as well as 𝐷 and 𝑌 .
The required condition also better matches the formal proof in [6].) Moreover, the theorem in [7]
clearly doesn’t contradict this proof, since the probability of an all-zeroes Hadamard transform

theorem. (Again, though, averaging over traced-out values of the 𝑏𝑖 ’s in set 𝐴 would produce a very
different—and incorrect—result in the successful all-zeroes-𝐴 case, as in the previous variation.)
   Finally, if we consider the original algorithm, in which set 𝐴 is post-selected (randomly among
sets of all-zeroes groups of samples, assuming a sufficient fixed number of groups processed), its
output can easily be shown to be identical to that of the variation in which 𝐴 is randomly pre-
selected, conditioned on the Hadamard transforms on 𝐴 in every case all producing zeroes, as hoped.
The only difference is the much larger success probability, which can thus be seen to be a direct
consequence of post-selecting 𝐴. It’s also consistent with the theorem proven in [7] in the sense
that whichever set 𝐴 is ultimately post-selected, the probability that that particular set produced
all zeroes when Hadamard-transformed—and the ℎ ∗ and ℎ computed from it thus deviate from the
distribution dictated by the traced-out-𝐴 density matrix—was a priori exponentially small, and thus
within the bound specified by the theorem. However, the exponentially small probabilities for each
choice of 𝐴 sum over the exponentially many choices of 𝐴 to produce a significant (overwhelming,
in fact) probability that at least one such choice is available to the algorithm, making success via
post-selction possible.
   To summarize, 1) the algorithm cannot work unless its output deviates non-negligibly from the
traced-out-𝐴 average, but can tolerate averaging over traced-out values of the 𝑏𝑖 ’s in B; 2) deviation
from the traced-out-𝐴 average can occur with high probability if set 𝐴 is Hadamard-transformed
and the result is all zeroes; and 3) post-selection of set 𝐴 allows that latter condition to hold with
high probability overall, rather than an exponentially small one. Tracing out all the 𝑏𝑖 ’s—including
those in 𝐴—as proposed in [7], will thus produce an incorrect prediction of the algorithm’s output.
