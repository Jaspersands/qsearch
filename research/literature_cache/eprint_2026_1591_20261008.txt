                                     Abstract
          We present a polynomial-time quantum algorithm for the Dihedral
      Coset Problem (DCP). The algorithm is based on Regev’s polynomial-
      time reduction of the Dihedral Subgroup Problem (DSP) to the modular
      subset sum problem [11], but uses a different technique to erase sample
      bits without use of a subset sum oracle. The algorithm can thus combine
      with Regev’s [11] reduction of lattice problems to DCP, improved
      by Brakerski, Kirshanova, Stehlé and Wen [3], to yield polynomial-
      time quantum algorithms for various lattice problems, such as finding
      a polynomial-factor approximation to the shortest vector in an n-
      dimensional lattice (SVP), and the “learning with errors” problem
      (LWE). The algorithm can tolerate a faulty sample rate as high as
      1/O(log n), allowing the algorithm-reduction
                                       √             combination to efficiently
      solve, for example, SVP with
                                 √   a   n polylog(n) approximation factor, or
      LWE instances with α = n polylog(n).


1     Introduction
1.1    Lattice-Based Cryptography
The discovery of quantum algorithms capable of efficiently attacking the
most popular classical public-key cryptosystems [13] has created interest in
public-key cryptosystems that are resistant to such quantum attacks. One
promising approach is “lattice cryptography” [9], which is based on the
believed computational difficulty of various problems over lattices, such as
the polynomial-factor approximate Shortest Vector Problem (p(n)-SVP).
p(n)-SVP is the problem of finding a non-zero vector in an n-dimensional

                                         1
lattice whose length is within a polynomial p(n) factor of the length of the
shortest non-zero vector in the lattice. The security of several lattice-based
cryptosystems, such as the Ajtai-Dwork cryptosystem [1] and “learning
with errors” (LWE) [12], depends on the hardness of p(n)-SVP. The best
previously available algorithms—classical or quantum—for solving p(n)-SVP,
such as BKZ ([4]), require fully exponential time.

1.2    The Dihedral Subgroup Problem
The Dihedral Subgroup Problem (DSP) [5] can be stated as follows: assume
that a function f is constant on a subgroup H of a dihedral group G, as
well as on each of its cosets; the problem is to identify H. Ettinger and
Høyer [5] showed that it suffices to solve the problem in the case where the
subgroup is of order 2. Applying the standard “sampling” method results
in the following very simple formulation of the problem, due to Regev [11]:
given a source of random samples of the superposition
                       1          1
                      √ |0, x⟩ + √ |1, x + d    (mod N )⟩
                        2          2

where 2N ≈ 2n+1 is the size of the dihedral group, d, x ∈ {0...N − 1}, d is
fixed and x is chosen arbitrarily for each sample, find d.
     Boneh and Lipton [2] showed that the general hidden subgroup problem
for Abelian groups is in quantum polynomial time, but dihedral groups are
(slightly) non-Abelian. The best previously known quantum algorithm   √       for
solving   DSP   is due  to Kuperberg   [7], and has  running  time 2O( log N ) ≈
    √
2O( n) . Regev [11] gives a polynomial-time quantum algorithm for DSP, but
it relies on a subset sum oracle to “erase” input bits. The algorithm presented
here emulates Regev’s algorithm, but handles the input bits differently.

1.3    The Dihedral Coset Problem
The Dihedral Coset Problem (DCP) [11] is identical to Regev’s formulation
of the DSP, but with a faulty sample probability of 1/a(n) in the DSP
samples—that is, with probability 1/a(n) that the sample will consist of a
random bit and random value, rather than the correct superposition. Regev
[11] showed a polynomial-time reduction from solving a(n)-SVP to solving
                                             2
DCP on a group of size 2N , where N ≈ 2n . This quadratic dimension
increase was later removed in an improved reduction from LWE presented by
Brakerski, Kirshanova, Stehlé and Wen [3]. These reductions both yield an
                          √
approximation factor a(n) n polylog(n), but at the cost of a 1/a(n) faulty


                                       2
sample probability. Kuperberg’s algorithm requires    √ error-free input, and
hence produces an approximation factor at best 2O( n) when combined with
Regev’s reduction—no better than what BKZ offers classically. Since the
quantum algorithm presented here tolerates a faulty sample rate as high as
1/O(log n), it can produce (via [3]), for example, a polynomial-time quantum
                                                    √
algorithm for SVP with an approximation factor npolylog(n), or for LWE
                    √
instances with α = n polylog(n) [10] [8].

      Finally, we compute and measure h′ = h ⊕ h∗ . Recall that h starts off
encoding dn in the Hadamard basis—that is, that the h = 0 and h = 1 states
have the same or opposite phases depending on whether dn = 0 or dn = 1. If
for each value of h′ the effect of Hadamard-transforming the bi ’s in set B on
the phases of h∗ = 0 and h∗ = 1 is the same, then it’s easy to verify that the
computation of h′ effectively copies h’s encoding of dn to h∗ . (Whether the
bits of h and h∗ are exactly the same or exactly opposite, the relationship
between the final phases of their two states will be the same for both qubits.)
We can thus use h′ and h∗ to erase h, leaving a single unmeasured bit h∗ ,
and measure h∗ in the Hadamard basis, in place of h, to obtain dn . All that
remains is to show that the effect of set B on the amplitudes and phases of
the states h∗ = 0 and h∗ = 1 is approximately equal for both.

3    A Note on [6]
Gupte, Ragavan and Zhandry [6] recently presented a proof that the density
matrix following step 2 in the above algorithm is exponentially close to that
which would be produced if all the qubits bi were traced out (i.e., measured
and then discarded). They argue that this result implies that the above
algorithm can’t possibly produce a correct value of dn with non-negligible
probability, since the information about dn is encoded in the relationship
between the phases of each value of h, and thus destroyed when all the bi ’s
are traced out, collapsing h to a single value.
    As it turns out, Lemmas 1, 2 and 3 are all completely compatible with the
result in [6]—Lemmas 2 and 3, in particular, focus on the ϕB portion of the
state, which could in fact be traced out, reducing it to a single value, without
affecting the algorithm at all. Rather, it’s Lemma 4—–the claim that the
ϕA state portions are roughly evenly distributed among z ∗ values—that fails
once the bits in ϕA are traced out.
    Now, this would certainly cause the algorithm to fail—set A is exactly
the set that needs to be represented for both values of h and h∗ , and hence
not be traced out, for the algorithm to work. However, the fact that A is
post-selected such that the results of the Hadamard transforms on its bits are
all zero allows the values of h and h∗ computed in the case where the values
of the bi ’s in A are traced out to diverge non-negligibly from the actual case,
despite the proof in [6].
    Consider, for example, the case where all the bi ’s are measured as zero
following their Hadamard transform. In that case, the algorithm works
correctly (as a mildly modified version of Regev’s original algorithm, with
the bi ’s just erased a different way), even though its pre-Hadamard-transform
density matrix, per [6], is only negligibly different from the one for the case
where the bi ’s were all traced out, preventing the algorithm from working
correctly. Of course, the Hadamard transform on the bi ’s only results in all


                                      15
zeroes with exponentially small probability. But note that that the probability
expressed in the density matrix in [6] is over choices of the yi ’s, and an all-
zeroes outcome of the Hadamard transforms makes the average output across
all yi ’s completely different from the all-bi ’s-traced-out case. In effect, the
Hadamard transform appears to shift the expected difference between the
intact-bi ’s and traced-out-bi ’s cases from one calculated over choices of the yi ’s
to one calculated over choices of the outcome of the Hadamard transforms.
     (In density matrix terms, the Hadamard transform shifts the negligible
difference between the two cases for a function h—or h∗ —calculated on the
bi ’s from one based on random sparse sets of bi vectors that produce small
off-diagonal terms in the density matrix to one based on random phases
imparted to the values of the function. This near-equivalence thus breaks
down when the result of the Hadamard transform is all zeroes, and therefore
imparts no phases to the function values.)
     Now, consider a second variation on the algorithm: pick a random set
A of n/(log n) groups of bi ’s, then measure the rest of the bi ’s, hoping that
the Hadamard transforms on set A all result in zeroes. Again, it’s easy to
verify that the algorithm works correctly in the (exponentially unlikely) case
that the hoped-for result occurs: ϕB is reduced to a single string with a
single z¯∗ (the lower n − 1 bits of z ∗ ), and hence as long as there are roughly
equally many ϕA ’s for both values of h∗ given the z¯∗ determined by the
measured ϕB , h∗ is approximately in the ”plus” state (equal amplitudes for
both values), and the phase on h is thus easily recovered. In contrast, if we
average over traced-out values of the bi ’s in set A, per [6], then the algorithm
clearly fails, since all the superpositions—including of h—collapse. So once
again, we have a non-negligible difference between the average results (over
yi values) of the algorithm in the traced-out-A case and the intact-A case, if
the Hadamard transforms on the bi ’s in A all result in zeroes—and moreover,
the result obtained in the intact-A case is in fact the correct one.
     Next, consider a third variation, identical to the previous one except that
we omit the measurement of the bi ’s in set B (still hoping that the Hadamard
transforms on the bi ’s in A all result in zeroes, and would form exactly the
set A chosen by the algorithm). Putting aside that the proof presented
in Section 2 also proves that the algorithm works in this variation if the
(exponentially unlikely) hope is realized, the argument in [6] can be applied
equally here to show that the algorithm works as correctly in this variation
as in the previous one. (In fact, we can view this argument as a roundabout
alternative proof of Lemma 3.) After all, based on the bit-by-bit argument in
[6], before set A has been Hadamard-transformed and measured to produce
all zeroes, the closeness of the density matrices in the cases where set B is

                                         16
pre-measured—as in the previous variation—and not pre-measured should
be neglibly different. (Again, though, extending the application of averaging
over traced-out values to set A as well would produce a very different—and
incorrect—result in the all-zeroes case, as in the previous variation.)
    Finally, if we consider the original algorithm, in which set A is post-
selected, its output will be identical to that of the previous variation in which
A is pre-selected, conditioned on the Hadamard transforms all coming out
as hoped—the only difference is the much larger success probability due to
post-selection. Note also that whatever set A is ultimately post-selected,
the probability that that particular set produced all zeroes when Hadamard-
transformed was a priori exponentially small, and thus nominally consistent
with the result in [6].
    So to summarize, 1) for the algorithm to work, we need the outcome to
deviate from the traced-out-A average, but we can tolerate averaging over
traced-out values of the bi ’s in B; 2) deviation from the traced-out-A average
can occur with high probability if set A is Hadamard-transformed and the
result is all zeroes; and 3) post-selection of set A allows that latter condition
to hold with high probability overall, rather than an exponentially small one.
Tracing out all the bi ’s, as proposed in [6], will thus produce an incorrect
prediction of the algorithm’s output.
