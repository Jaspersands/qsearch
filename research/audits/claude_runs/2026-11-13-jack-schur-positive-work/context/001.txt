    In a sudden quench [7] the drive is instantaneous at t = 0: we set τ = 0+ and evolve
for a time t under Hf . If the prepared state is an eigenstate, Hi |ψ0 ⟩ = E0 |ψ0 ⟩ (the
usual situation in our applications: a translation-invariant ground state, or a domain-wall

                                        G(t) := ⟨ψ0 |e−iHf t |ψ0 ⟩                                  (7)
is the Loschmidt amplitude and L(t) = |G(t)|2 the Loschmidt echo. Thus P (w) is the Fourier
transform of a Loschmidt-type object, while L(t) quantifies fidelity decay [1, 2, 7, 8, 16, 17].
This connection has driven much of the modern activity on DQPTs, where nonanalytic
features in G(t) govern far-from-equilibrium behavior [8, 9]. On the experimental side,
interferometric protocols access χW (u) directly via an ancilla, enabling reconstructions of
P (w) and tests of fluctuation relations in a variety of platforms [4–6].

Why random matrices enter: Toeplitz determinants and trace statistics
The key structural observation we develop and exploit is that, for sudden quenches in
quadratic (or effectively free) spin/fermion models with periodic boundary conditions (PBC),
the Loschmidt amplitude admits a Toeplitz (or block-Toeplitz) determinant representation
whose symbol is built from the post-quench single-particle dispersion ε(k) [18, 19]. Toeplitz
determinants are exactly equivalent to partition functions of unitary random-matrix
ensembles via the Heine–Szegő identity [20, 21]. In the present context, this equivalence
upgrades the work problem to a random-matrix linear-statistics problem and allows us to
import sharp probabilistic tools.
    In the settings studied here one has a Toeplitz representation GN (t) = DN (e−it ε ), and
therefore χW (u) = e−iuE0 DN (eiuε ). The deterministic phase from the zero Fourier mode
only shifts the mean and will often be suppressed; the nontrivial factor is a characteristic
function of a random-matrix linear statistic (Sec. III). Concretely, expanding the dispersion
in Fourier modes,
