Prior work. The barrier is related to four earlier work.
1. Preconditioned block encodings: Lapworth et al. [11] report it numerically for preconditioned quantum linear
   solvers.
2. Inverse-Laplacian preconditioning: Golden et al. [10] precondition hydrological flow, a scalar elliptic problem
   of the type treated here, by the inverse Laplacian, and find that its norm grows linearly in the number of unknowns,
   so that its block encoding carries the mesh dependence it removes classically.
3. FFT-based fixed-point iteration: Liu et al. [8] implement the iteration of Moulinec and Suquet [18] for the cell
   problem with quantum Fourier transforms, encoding each of its 𝑆 steps as a separate non-unitary map. The 𝑆 steps
   form a polynomial of degree 𝑆 in the preconditioned operator, composing them multiplies their subnormalizations,
   and the success probability of the composed circuit is not analyzed.
 4. Wavelet preconditioning: Bagherimehrab et al. [27] and Gharat et al. [28] transform the operator to a wavelet
    basis and apply a diagonal preconditioner, which bounds the condition number of the preconditioned matrix
    independently of 𝑁. The cost of QSVT depends instead on the condition number seen by the circuit, which includes
    the subnormalization of the preconditioner and, for factors encoded separately, is bounded below by Equation (16)
    [12].
    Deiml and Peterseim [29] avoid the barrier by building the preconditioner into the encoding, with a multilevel
construction that does not require periodicity, at a subnormalization that grows with the fine-scale resolution and with
the coefficient contrast. A preconditioner therefore lowers the cost of QSVT only if it is built into the block encoding
of the preconditioned operator, not multiplied onto an encoding of 𝐊𝜒 ; such an encoding is built in Section 3.

3.1. Fast Inversion
    Tong et al. [30] introduced fast inversion in the context of quantum preconditioning. A matrix is fast-invertible
if a block encoding of its inverse can be built at a cost independent of its condition number. This holds when the
matrix is diagonalized by a quantum Fourier transform and its eigenvalues are known in closed form [30, Prop. 2].
The homogeneous matrix 𝐊 is of this kind (Section 2.2). For an operator 𝐀 + 𝐁 with 𝐀 fast-invertible, Tong et al.
apply QSVT to the preconditioned operator 𝐼 + 𝐀−1 𝐁, with a number of queries that grows with the product of the
subnormalizations of 𝐀−1 and 𝐁 [30, Thm. 1]. This product does not grow with the mesh when 𝐁 is a lower-order term,
such as a bounded potential added to the Laplacian [30].
    However, this approach fails for the two-phase operator. In the natural splitting, 𝐀 = 𝜎min 𝐊 is the operator of a cell
made entirely of the soft matrix, and 𝐁 = 𝐊𝜒 − 𝜎min 𝐊 is the additional stiffness contributed by the inclusion. Unlike
that of a potential, the norm of 𝐁 is proportional to the largest eigenvalue of 𝐊, while the norm of 𝐀−1 is set by the
smallest nonzero eigenvalue of 𝐊. Their product grows as Θ(𝑁 2 ), and the query count of [30, Thm. 1] grows at least
in proportion to 𝑁 2 , as in Equation (16).
    In this paper, we use fast inversion for 𝐊 in a different way. Instead of preconditioning from the left, we apply
(𝐊+ )1∕2 on both sides and absorb it into the factors of 𝐊𝜒 (Section 3.2). The preconditioner is then never encoded as
a separate factor, and the preconditioned operator is encoded with subnormalization 1, and it is inverted by QSVT in
Section 3.7.

   Equation (28) has the form of an FFT-based classical solver: a transform to wavenumbers, one small operation per

the 𝑁 2 nodes. The output 𝐕𝒖 is a gradient field, with eight values at each of the 𝑁 2 elements: two gradient components
at each of four Gauss points. Each value is therefore identified by a position, a node or an element, and by a local index.

each. The position register has 𝑚 = log2 𝑁 2 qubits and holds the index 𝒋 of a node or an element. The local register
holds the local index 𝓁. A nodal vector is stored in local state |0⟩, with its 𝑁 2 values in the position register, and a
gradient field in local states |0⟩ to |7⟩, where |2𝑔 − 2⟩ holds the 𝑥-gradient and |2𝑔 − 1⟩ the 𝑦-gradient at Gauss point 𝑔.

6. Conclusions
    Placing the closed-form inverse square root of the homogeneous operator inside the factors of the periodic two-
phase operator gives a block encoding of the preconditioned operator with subnormalization 1 and condition number
at most 𝜌 for every 𝑁. The mesh enters the cost only through the quantum Fourier transforms and the wavenumber-
controlled stage, both polylogarithmic in 𝑁, and the condition number, which is the quantity that sets the number of
queries, is a material constant. This is the property that classical FFT-preconditioned solvers rely on, and it does not
survive a separately encoded preconditioner.
    The construction is limited to what makes 𝐊 fast-invertible: a uniform periodic mesh, a homogeneous reference
operator with closed-form eigenvalues, and isotropic phases, whose conductivities are scalar multiples of that of the
reference; Dirichlet boundaries and general coefficients require the multilevel construction compared in Section 3.6.
For isotropic phases of contrast 𝜌, the degree of the inverse grows linearly in 𝜌: at 𝜖𝑝 = 10−6 , Equation (36) gives
𝑑𝑡 = 145 at 𝜌 = 10 and 𝑑𝑡 ≤ 1.5 × 105 at 𝜌 = 104 , although 𝑑𝑡 does not grow with 𝑁. The cost of each of these
queries is that of the fixed-point arithmetic that prepares the vector at each wavenumber, and the precision it needs
grows with 𝑁, with 𝑑𝑡 , and with the subnormalization 𝐶 (Equation (42)): at 𝑁 = 1024 and 𝜖𝑝 = 10−6 , 𝐹 = 45
fractional bits and 1.3 × 107 Toffoli gates for one application of the inverse. Up to 𝑁 = 2048 in two dimensions, a
table lookup of classically computed angles prepares the same vectors with fewer T gates; the arithmetic is the method
whose cost grows with 𝑁 only through 𝐹 . The arithmetic needs too many qubits for a state-vector simulation, so the
simulation of Section 5.5 builds the rotation at each wavenumber as a multiplexor, whose size grows as 𝑁 2 ; it confirms
that the stages compose into the inverse, and the arithmetic is verified separately, one stage at a time. The circuit cost
reported here excludes the synthesis of its rotations into Clifford and T gates and leaves the qubit count of the reversible
arithmetic unoptimized. Finally, we provide the operator and its inverse as block encodings: the inverse prepares a state
proportional to the solution, and extracting the field from it is not addressed. The effective conductivity is a quadratic
form that does not require the field, and its readout is the subject of the companion paper. Even with that readout,
classical solvers are vastly superior for problems of practical interest.
