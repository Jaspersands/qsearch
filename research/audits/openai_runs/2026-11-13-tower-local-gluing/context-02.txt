                          3. Algorithmic prerequisites
   The algorithms presented in this paper depend on the number of already existing
tools. First of all, we need routines for solving systems of linear equations (basically
over F2 ) and for basic arithmetic in a global ﬁeld (see, e.g., [10]). As far as more
advanced tools are concerned, we need:
   (1) Factorization of ideals in global ﬁelds (see, e.g., [10, 17]).
   (2) Solution to a norm equation of a form NL/K (x) = b, where b ∈ K × and
L/K is a ﬁnite ﬁeld extension. As was mentioned in the introduction, this problem
is discussed in [11, 14, 15, 16, 27, 37].
   (3) Construction of a basis (over F2 ) of the group ES of S-singular square classes.
The author is aware of three existing methods. One is described in [21]. Another
one is outlined in the Magma manual [7]. In this method, one constructs a set T
containing S, such that the T -class number is odd. Then, it is known that the

groups ET and UT coincide. Hence, the sought group ES can be constructed as a
subspace of UT . A third method, due to A. Czogała, was recently described in [22].

                                     4. Algorithms
    In this section, we present the main results of the paper, namely the algorithms
for constructing an isotropic vector of a given quadratic form. Our general strategy
is as follows. For forms of dimensions 2 or 3, the corresponding vector is constructed
directly. On the other hand, for higher dimensional forms, we will reduce the
problem to the one for forms of lower dimension. Hence, we will recursively push
it down the ladder till we reach ternary forms. Unfortunately, the strategy diﬀers
signiﬁcantly from one dimension to the other. Consequently, we present dedicated

Remark 3. In order to rigorously prove that after ﬁnitely many iterations, we will
come across a prime q satisfying assertions of Proposition 4.5, we should exhaus-
tively iterate over all the primes. If K is a number ﬁeld, we may, for example, start
with a prime number p = 3 and loop over all the primes of K dividing p. Then
take p = 5 and again loop over all the primes of K over p, and so on. A similar
exhaustive search can be applied to global function ﬁelds, as well. In practice, a
much more eﬃcient solution is just to pick q at random, using the fact that the set
of such primes has a positive density (see the previous remark). Thus, depending
on the selected strategy for ﬁnding primes in step (5i) the above algorithm can be
viewed either as a deterministic or a randomized one.

                   Appendix A. Complexity of Algorithm 1
   A detailed complexity analysis of algorithms presented in this paper seems to
be very diﬃcult, and deﬁnitely exceeds the competence of the author. One of the
reasons is that to the best of our knowledge not all of the external subroutines, we
rely on, oﬀer such a rigorous complexity analysis. In this appendix we provide a
crude estimate for the time complexity of Algorithm 1.
   Let K be a global ﬁeld represented either in the form K = Q(θ), if K is a
number ﬁeld, or K = Fpk (x, θ), if it is a function ﬁeld. Denote the monic minimal
polynomial of θ by f and let d be the degree of f . An element of K can be expressed
as a linear combination of successive powers of θ, say a = a0 + a1 θ + · · · + ad−1θd−1 .
Deﬁne the size of a to be the sum of bit-sizes of a0 , . . . , ad−1 . For a quadratic form q
over K, deﬁne its size as a sum of sizes of its coeﬃcients.
   In order to construct the set P(q) in step (2) of the algorithm, one has to factor
the principal ideals generated by K-integral representatives of square classes of
coeﬃcients of q. If K is a number ﬁeld, this problem can be reduced in polynomial
time to the problem of factoring integers (see, e.g., [11, §2.3.5]). The best known
algorithms for the latter task are sub-exponential in size of the input. GNFS (see,
e.g., [6], [26]) has complexity
                      √         Ln (1/3, (64/9)1/3 ), while ECM (see, e.g., [29], [19]) has
complexity Lp (1/2, 2), where p is the smallest prime dividing the integer to be
factored. Now, the computation of a norm increases the size of the input by the
factor |K : Q| and so the complexity of step (2) for number ﬁelds is sub-exponential
in the size of the form and the degree of the ﬁeld.
   If K is a global function ﬁeld, a ﬁnite extension of Fpk (x), then the problem
of factoring the ideals can be analogously reduced to factoring polynomials over
a ﬁnite ﬁeld. The later problem is well-known to be polynomial-time in the size
of the input (see, e.g., [43, Section 14.8]) Hence, for function ﬁelds step (2) has
polynomial time in size of the input and the degree d.
   Now, steps (3–4) are meaningful only for formally real ﬁelds (hence, necessarily
number ﬁelds). They boil down to performing a real root isolation of polynomials
with rational coeﬃcients. This can be done in polynomial time with respect to the
degree |K : Q| and the logarithm of the root distance. For details we refer the
reader to [1, Section 10.2] (see also [2, Section 3]).
   As expected, it is the analysis of the loop in step (5) which is most involving.
The construction in step (5a) of the basis B from scratch during the ﬁrst execution
of the loop can be reduced in polynomial time (with respect to the size of S)
to the problem of computing the S-unit group. For a general number ﬁeld, the
author is not aware of any algorithm for a latter task that would oﬀer a complexity
better than exponential in the size of S and the bit-size of the discriminant of K.
Related sub-exponential algorithms (see, e.g., [3]) rely on some heuristics. On the
other hand, as explained in Remark 1, in subsequent iterations of the loop, after
expanding S, the basis B can be updated incrementally in time polynomial with
respect to the size of S and the class number of K. The corresponding algorithm for
function ﬁelds is described in [21]. It is likely that one can use a similar incremental
algorithm also for number ﬁelds. However, to the best of our knowledge no such
algorithm has ever been published.

   Alternatively, we may treat Algorithm 1 as a deterministic one, exhaustively
iterating over the primes of K. Although it may be tempting to base our analysis
in this setting on an eﬀective version of the Chebotarev Density Theorem (see, e.g.,

we are not interested in the number of primes in Q, whose norms sit in a given
interval. What we need is to estimate how quickly we will ﬁnd the ﬁrst prime
(with respect to the order in which we scan them) sitting in Q. This is sometimes
referred to as the “ﬁrst prime problem”. It is known to work in polynomial time
with respect to the discriminant of K by [24] (see also [20] and citations within).
Consequently, step (5) runs in the deterministic polynomial time with respect to
the the degree of K, its discriminant and size of the coeﬃcients, provided that
the ﬁrst construction of the basis B is moved to the initialization phase (see the
comments of the end of the previous paragraph).
   Finally, let us look at step (7). Here, we need to ﬁnd isotropic vectors of two
ternary forms. As explained in Section 4.1, this problem is equivalent to solving
two norm equations. Algorithms devoted to this task has been recalled in the
introduction. For number ﬁelds, Simon’s algorithm [37] seems to be the most
popular one. (It can be also adapted to function ﬁelds.) The author is not aware
of any complete rigorous complexity analysis of Simon’s algorithm. It is claimed
(without proof) in [4] that over cyclotomic ﬁelds a solution of the norm equation
can be computed in probabilistic polynomial time with respect to the bit-size of the
input, provided that the factorization of the input is known. The latter condition
can be safely assumed in our case since the set S is given and we are interested only
in solutions modulo squares. Yet still, this claim refers only to cyclotomic ﬁelds.
Conversely, in [31] there is a complete complexity analysis of a stripped down version
