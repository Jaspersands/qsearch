The algorithm we propose is inspired by Gaussian elimination. More formally, we introduce a practical
hypergraph-cycle-enumeration hierarchy for planted noisy k-XOR that retains the smooth trade-off of
Kikuchi-type methods, while reducing recovery at every level to spectral estimation on an n × n matrix.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
• We introduce hypergraph cycle enumeration (HCE), a hierarchical algorithmic approach to planted
  noisy k-XOR (Section IV). On level l, HCE aggregates sets of l clauses, each giving a weak parity signal,
  into an n × n recovery matrix. Crucially, unlike Kikuchi, whose level-ℓ operator acts on a lifted space
  of dimension nℓ , HCE keeps the spectral problem in the original variable space, with matrix size
                   
  independent of l.
• We provide detection and recovery guarantees based respectively on collecting size-l even covers,
  and using size-l sets of clauses with symmetric difference of size 2 to generate a dense n × n matrix, the
  principal component of which aligns with the solution. For both detection and recovery, the parameter
  l traces a similar threshold curve as in the Kikuchi and SoS hierarchies: increasing l combines more
  clauses at higher computational cost. Specifically, HCE succeeds at the same threshold number of
  clauses per variable, m/n = e  Ω(ρ−2 (n/l)k/2−1 ). Thus, the HCE hierarchy exhibits a smooth trade-
  off, joining SoS, Kikuchi and the gadget-based methods reviewed in Section III as hierarchies with this
  property.
• We compare the complexity of HCE to the best competitor, the Kikuchi method. At matched even
  levels and k = 4, their stated runtime bounds have the same leading exponent with the improved HCE
  probing rule, while HCE uses a smaller matrix representation. We run experiments for k = 4, 5, 6
  (Section VI) using the original all-pairs probe, showing that HCE matches Kikuchi recovery overlap at
  equal level while using substantially less memory and runtime, especially in the ultra-sparse regimes
  (e.g., at n = 50, k = 4, level 6, HCE completes in ≈300 s versus ≈5.5 h per instance for Kikuchi, a ∼60×
  speed-up; Appendix G). Thus HCE presents a practical advantage, being able to solve some instances
  beyond the capability of Kikuchi, and the advantage is largest in the hardest (sparsest) regime.
   Note: for recovery, we use smooth trade-off in the soft-asymptotic sense common in work on the Kikuchi
hierarchy, where thresholds are compared after suppressing logarithmic factors [16]. Our detection result
(Theorem 3) is stronger: it has no logarithmic loss. We conjecture that a normalized or suitably trimmed
HCE recovery operator removes the logarithmic slack, uniformly over a non-trivial range of growing levels
l = l(n); proving this remains open.

                                 V.    THEORETICAL GUARANTEES

   Theorems 3 and 4 lay the theoretical foundation of detection and recovery via HCE. These are the
two main results that we will state in this section, and defer complete proofs to Appendices B and D.
   The literature on planted inference problems uses a number of different models for how the clauses
are sampled. For convenience and clarity of derivation, we consider two models. For detection, H is a
uniformly random m-edge subhypergraph of K: the supports are drawn uniformly without replacement,
so H is a simple hypergraph and even covers are sets of distinct edges. This model was used in Ref. [6].
For recovery, S1 , . . . , Sm are i.i.d. uniform k-subsets, so repeated supports may occur and witnesses are
indexed by clause indices rather than by edges; repeated supports are retained rather than discarded.
This model or its Poissonization were used in Refs. [4, 14, 16, 22]. In practice the two models can be seen
to be equivalent up to constants factors. Given a Poissonized instance with m̂ variables, E[m̂] = m, with
high probability m̂ = (1 + o(1))m and so asymptotically the regimes are equivalent. The Poissonized
instance may have repeated clauses; in that case one can deduplicate the clauses, again with a subleading
loss in the regime of interest m = Θ(nk/2 ).
